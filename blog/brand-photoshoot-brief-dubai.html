How to Brief a Brand Photoshoot in Dubai:
A Template for Brands and Models

Written by Hatim
Published November 2026
Read time 7 min

“We need some clean photos for our launch” is a good opening message. It tells me you have something worth photographing. What it doesn’t tell me yet is whether we’re making a campaign, photographing a collection for your website, or trying to do both before the same deadline.

Those are different shoots. A campaign image might need movement, a strong expression and enough space for a headline. A product page needs the garment to be easy to see, with consistent angles across the collection. The same model might do both brilliantly, but the direction on set will change.

A good brief makes those decisions easier on any brand photoshoot in Dubai. It gives the photographer enough information to plan and quote, and gives the model enough information to decide whether the job is right for them. You can write one in a few paragraphs. Here’s what to include.

1. Start with what the images need to do

Before sending a mood board, finish this sentence: “We need these images so that…”

A Dubai fashion label might need customers to see how a new collection moves and fits. A founder might need portraits that introduce the person behind the business. A hospitality brand might need lifestyle photographs that show what it feels like to spend an afternoon at its property. Each of those goals points to a different shot list, even if all three briefs use the words clean, natural and premium.

If you share references, say what you like about each one. “We like the soft light in the first image and the way the clothes move in the second” is more useful than a folder of twenty images with no notes. It also leaves room to make something that looks like your brand.

2. Decide where the photos will appear

Tell your photographer where you plan to use the images: your website, product pages, organic social posts, paid ads, email, print or outdoor displays. If you’re unsure, say what you know now and flag what might come later.

This matters for the photographs themselves. A horizontal website banner may need clear space for copy on one side. A vertical Story needs a composition that works on a phone. A full-length image for a product page needs room around the model’s feet. When those formats are in the brief, we can compose for them on set and check the crops before moving on.

It also matters for the people in the images. Ask for the planned channels, territory and duration to be agreed in writing. If paid advertising or category exclusivity is part of the plan, bring it up before confirming the booking. As a model, if the brief just says “social media,” a useful follow-up is: “Is that organic posting only, or will the images also run as paid ads?”

3. Cast for what the model needs to do

There’s a difference between liking someone’s portfolio and knowing what you need them to do on the day. A beauty close-up calls for control in small expressions. A lookbook needs repeatable poses and clear garment presentation. A lifestyle campaign might ask two people who’ve just met to look comfortable together in front of the camera.

Put that in the brief. Say how many people you need, what they’ll be doing, what they’ll wear and whether there’s dialogue, movement or video. If sample garments are already made, share the relevant sizes early. If wardrobe or actions may affect someone’s comfort, disclose them before asking anyone to accept.

For models, your experience level doesn’t change what you’re entitled to ask about the shoot. Whether it’s your first commercial job or your fiftieth, it’s reasonable to clarify the role, wardrobe, hours, fee and intended image use. If the brief says “lifestyle talent,” ask what that looks like on set. Walking through a hotel lobby, appearing with another model and speaking to camera are three different jobs.

4. Make a short list of shots you can’t leave without

Start with the images the launch depends on. For a fashion collection, that might be one campaign hero, a full-length image of every look and close-ups of two details that make the collection distinctive. Put the extra ideas underneath.

An example of a useful instruction: “For the opening look, we need a horizontal banner with space for the headline, plus a separate vertical frame for Stories.” That gives the model and photographer a much clearer job than “Shoot some landscape and portrait.”

Dubai belongs in the brief when it serves the idea. A studio may be right for a collection that needs consistent colour and angles. A location may be right because its architecture, light or atmosphere tells part of the story. If you’ve picked a location, name it. If it’s provisional, flag that too, along with any access arrangements, travel between locations or backup plan that could affect the schedule. 5. Give the shoot a deadline and name who makes decisions

Include a preferred shoot window and the date you actually need the finished images. If the launch is on Friday but your designer needs the files on Tuesday, Tuesday is the delivery deadline. Mention whether you need a few priority frames early, and who will approve the selects and retouching.

If you have a budget range, share it. It helps me suggest a scope that makes sense. If you don’t, tell me what you can’t compromise on. One location and fewer finished images may give you a stronger result than squeezing several setups into the same day.

For models, ask who your contact is on set, when you’re expected to arrive and wrap, and who to speak to if something changes. A brief is useful because it gives everyone a shared point of reference when the day gets busy.

What it looks like filled in: Imagine a Dubai label launching a six-look collection. The team needs a horizontal homepage hero with room for copy, a vertical launch image and a consistent full-length frame for each look. The images are for the UAE website and organic social; paid ads are still undecided. The brand has the clothes and a stylist but needs help with casting and finding a studio. The designer needs the hero image before the rest of the set. That’s enough to start a useful conversation about the shoot, even without a final location or budget.

If you’re the model receiving the brief

Before confirming, check that you know who the client is, what you’ll be doing and wearing, where and when you’re needed, how the images will be used, and what you’ll be paid and when. Ask about exclusivity if it comes up. If a detail affects your availability, comfort or ability to take other work, clarify it in writing before accepting.

You can keep the message simple:

Thanks for sending this over. Before I confirm, could you share the expected hours, wardrobe and where the final images will be used? I’d also like to confirm the fee and payment timing so we’re aligned before the shoot.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a finished mood board to enquire about a photoshoot in Dubai?
No. A short description of what you’re launching, where the images will appear and when you need them is enough to start. A few references help if you can explain what you like about them.
Can we shoot a lookbook and campaign images on the same day?
Yes, if the number of looks, setups and deliverables fits the schedule. List the two sets of images separately in the brief so the campaign frames get dedicated time.
Can a model ask how their photos will be used before accepting?
Yes. Ask about channels, territory, duration, paid advertising and any exclusivity. If the proposed use is still being decided, ask which uses are included in the current brief and how any additions will be agreed.
Do I need to book the photographer and the models together?
No. You can bring your own talent to a Photoczaro shoot, enquire about casting support, or browse Photoczaro Models if your photography team is already in place.
