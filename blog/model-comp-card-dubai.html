Model Comp Card in Dubai:
What to Include and How Many Looks

Written by Hatim
Published August 2026
Read time 7 min

A comp card is the smallest, most compact piece of material a model carries, and it's also the one most people get wrong first: either by cramming too much onto it, or by treating it as a replacement for a real portfolio.

If you've been asked for a comp card and aren't sure what that actually means, or you already have one and suspect it's doing you no favours, this covers what a comp card is for, what belongs on it, and how it fits alongside your digitals and portfolio rather than duplicating them.

What a comp card actually is

A comp card, short for composite card and also called a zed card, is a single sheet, printed or digital, showing a small selection of your strongest images next to your name, measurements and contact or agency details. It exists to be handed over, emailed or dropped into a casting folder in seconds.

That's the whole job of the thing: a fast, accurate summary. It is not where an agency or client goes to properly evaluate your range, that's what your portfolio is for. And it's not where they check what you currently look like, that's your digitals. A comp card sits downstream of both: it's built from images you've already had approved, compressed into something quick to reference.

Where a comp card fits

Digitals show what you look like right now. A portfolio shows what you can do. A comp card is the shortest possible version of both, built for speed, not depth.

What goes on the card

Layouts vary by agency and market, but most comp cards follow the same basic structure:

  • A strong lead image on the front, usually a clean headshot or three-quarter shot
  • Three to five supporting images on the back, chosen to show range rather than repeat the same expression or angle
  • Your name, clearly readable
  • Core measurements: height, bust or chest, waist, hips, shoe size, hair and eye colour
  • Contact details or agency information, depending on who the card is representing

The images should look like the same person across every frame. A comp card mixing a heavily edited beauty shot with a raw phone digital reads as inconsistent, and inconsistency is exactly what a card is supposed to eliminate.

How many looks do you actually need

One strong lead image plus three to five supporting shots covers most cards. More than that and the card stops being a quick reference and starts competing with your portfolio, which defeats the point. If an agency has issued you their own template, follow their count exactly rather than guessing.

A useful spread across those three to five images looks something like this: one clean headshot, one full-length shot showing proportions, one showing a different expression or energy, and one or two showing whatever range is relevant to the work you're pursuing, editorial, commercial, fitness, or otherwise.

Do you need a new shoot for this

Usually not. A comp card is normally built from images you already have, pulled from a recent digitals or portfolio session, not shot specifically for the card itself. If your existing material is honest, current and varied enough to give four or five different-looking frames, you likely already have what you need.

Where a dedicated session does make sense is if your current images are all too similar, all one expression, one outfit, one angle, and you genuinely don't have enough range to build a card that shows anything beyond a single note.

Comp card design: keep it simple

The temptation is to make the layout do more work than it should: multiple fonts, heavy graphics, a background that competes with the images. None of that helps. The images and the measurements are the content; the layout's only job is to present both cleanly and get out of the way.

  1. One clear hierarchy. Lead image biggest, supporting images consistent in size, text legible at a glance.
  2. Consistent colour and tone across images. Wildly different editing styles on the same card read as unedited inconsistency, not range.
  3. Accurate, current measurements. A comp card with outdated stats is worse than no comp card, since it sets the wrong expectation before you've even shown up.
  4. Legible contact or agency details. The card is useless if whoever holds it can't act on it.

A comp card is a small piece of material, but it's usually the first thing that gets picked up, glanced at and either kept or set aside. Getting it right is less about design skill and more about having honest, varied images to build it from in the first place, which is where the actual photography does the work.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a model comp card?
A comp card, short for composite card and also called a zed card, is a single printed or digital sheet showing a small selection of your strongest images alongside your name, measurements and contact or agency details. It is a quick-reference summary, not your full portfolio.
How many photos should be on a comp card?
Most comp cards use one strong lead image on the front and three to five supporting images on the back, chosen to show range rather than repeat the same angle or expression. Agencies with their own template may specify an exact count.
What is the difference between a comp card and a portfolio?
A portfolio is a full, curated collection of your work. A comp card is a compact summary built from a small number of already-approved portfolio or digitals images, designed for quick sharing at castings rather than in-depth viewing.
Do I need professional photos for a comp card?
The images on a comp card are usually pulled from your existing digitals or portfolio rather than shot specifically for the card itself, so the photography quality only needs to be as good as the source material you already have approved.
What measurements go on a model comp card?
Typically height, bust or chest, waist, hips, shoe size and hair and eye colour, though the exact fields depend on the market and category you are working in. Ask your agency which measurements they want listed if you are represented.
Can I make my own comp card without an agency?
Yes, many independent models design their own comp card, particularly for direct client work or before signing with an agency. Keep the layout simple and the images honest and current, since it will be judged the same way an agency-issued card would be.
