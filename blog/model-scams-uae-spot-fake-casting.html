Model Scams in the UAE:
How to Spot a Fake Casting or Agency Before You Pay

Written by Hatim
Published October 2026
Read time 17 min

AI-generated illustration (not a photograph of a real model): two phones side by side, a rushed message asking for payment and a calm one with the date, rate and payment terms laid out, while a hand signs only the clear contract.

Most model scams do not look like scams. They look like a casting, an agency that wants to sign you, or a brand that loves your Instagram. The money request comes later, wrapped in a reason that sounds routine.

I am Hatim, a Dubai portrait and model portfolio photographer, and I work with models at every stage. I have written this guide so you can recognise the common patterns, check an offer in a few minutes, and know exactly what to do if something already went wrong. It is aimed at models who live in the UAE and at models travelling here for the season.

Two honest limits. I am a photographer, not a lawyer or a police officer, so where rules matter I link the source and tell you to confirm with the authority involved. And I have no statistics on how many models are scammed, so I will not invent any. What follows are patterns that UAE authorities warn about and patterns that models describe, set out so you can test an offer against them.

The short answer

A real casting or agency does not need money from you before it books or represents you, does not ask you to work on a visit visa, does not keep your original passport, and puts the client, the job, the rate, the usage and the payment date in writing. If an offer fails any of these, stop and verify before you reply again.

Nine scams worth knowing

One red flag on its own is not always proof of a scam, but a request for money, secrecy or your documents deserves a pause every time. Dubai Police describe the basic pattern in plain terms: fraudulent ads and messages offer jobs, residency or visit visas in exchange for money, spread through social media and messaging apps, often under the name of an unlicensed company or a body posing as an official one (Gulf News report on the Dubai Police warning). Modeling versions of this are the nine below.

1. The pay-to-join agency

What it looks like. An agency tells you that you have “the look” and asks for a registration, signing, portfolio-listing or “profile activation” fee. It may be framed as a refundable deposit.

Why it works. Being told you are special is persuasive, and the fee is small compared with the dream. Once you have paid, the agency has its income whether or not you ever work.

What to do. Remember how legitimate agencies earn: a commission on bookings they find for you. UAE labour rules also place recruitment costs on the employer, not the worker (Gulf News explanation of recruitment costs). Ask in writing what you are paying for and what happens if you get no work. If the answer is vague, walk away. My note on approaching Dubai agencies explains what a normal approach looks like.

2. The fake job or visa offer

What it looks like. A message offers a modeling job in Dubai and a visa, then asks for money for visa processing, medical tests, training, a background check or an “entry permit”. Sometimes you are told to visit a travel office or to send the fees through a money-transfer service.

Why it works. It targets people abroad who want to come here for the season and cannot easily check the details. After the payment, the offer and the sender disappear. Dubai Police warn that visa matters should go through authorised government channels or legally approved offices only, and that you should avoid intermediaries and unverified parties (Gulf News report on the Dubai Police warning). Abu Dhabi Police describe the same method and note that recruitment companies cannot charge job seekers (Abu Dhabi Police notice).

What to do. Never pay a stranger to secure a job. MOHRE says job seekers should never agree to pay to secure a job, and that a genuine UAE job offer comes with an official offer letter issued through its system (Gulf News report on the MOHRE advice). Check the offer as described in the verification section below.

3. The mandatory photographer or “portfolio package”

What it looks like. An agency or “talent scout” says you cannot be considered without professional photos, then sends you to one photographer, often with a high package price and a deadline.

Why it works. Models do need good photographs, so the claim sounds reasonable. The problem is the lack of choice and the pressure, and sometimes the link between the agency and the photographer.

What to do. A paid shoot can be completely legitimate, and photographers deserve to be paid. It becomes a red flag when paying is a condition of being signed, when you must use one named photographer, or when you are rushed. Ask what you would receive, how many images, which retouching and what usage. My guides to digitals vs portfolio and to what a photoshoot involves will help you judge what you actually need. At Photoczaro, applying to the roster is free and paid photography is never a condition.

4. The impersonated brand, agency or photographer

What it looks like. You receive a message from an account that looks like a well-known brand, agency or photographer. The logo is right, the profile is polished, and the username is one character off the real one. It offers a paid campaign and asks you to confirm by clicking a link or paying a small amount.

Why it works. Copying a logo takes minutes, and a flattering message from a famous name lowers your guard.

What to do. Do not reply inside the message. Go to the real company’s website, find the contact details yourself and ask whether the offer exists. Check the exact username, the account age and whether real clients tag it. A verification badge alone is not proof. Report the lookalike account to the platform.

5. The payment trick

What it looks like. A “client” offers to pay and then sends too much, asking you to refund the difference. Or you are asked to pay shipping, insurance, courier or “contract” fees to receive products or a payment. Or the payment page you are sent looks like a bank.

Why it works. You feel you are being paid, so refunding a difference seems fair. Transfers can be reversed or fake after you have returned real money.

What to do. Never send money to receive money. Do not refund an overpayment until your own bank confirms the funds are settled, and only deal through your bank’s own app or site. If a real client owes you an amount, they pay you. You do not pay them first.

6. The private-images request and the blackmail that can follow

What it looks like. A casting asks for nude, lingerie or “private” test images, a video call, or a meeting alone in an apartment or hotel room, with no named client. In the worst cases the images are later used to threaten you or to demand money.

Why it works. The request is introduced as a normal casting step, then secrecy and shame do the rest.

What to do. Swimwear and lingerie are real categories in commercial work, so the category itself is not the warning. The warning is the setting and the order of events: no named client, no written brief, no contract, pressure, secrecy, a private location. Ask for the brief, the client and the usage in writing first. If anyone threatens you, do not pay and do not send more. You are not at fault. Keep the messages and report it (see the reporting section).

7. The contest, course or “model ID card”

What it looks like. You are invited to a “model of the year” contest, a modeling course, a workshop, a certificate or a membership card, all with an entry fee, and told it will lead to agency contact.

Why it works. It sells belonging and status, and often the event is real, which makes it feel safe.

What to do. You do not need a certificate or ID card to model, and clients do not ask for one. Courses can have value as training, but they are training, not a route to work. Ask who actually books models from the event, and buy only what you would be happy with if no work ever followed.

8. The document and data grab

What it looks like. Before any booking, you are asked for a passport copy, Emirates ID, bank details or a selfie holding your ID, often through a form on an unfamiliar site or a chat app.

Why it works. Paperwork sounds official, and a form looks like a process. The real purpose can be identity theft or a base for later fraud.

What to do. Share only what is needed, at the stage it is needed, with a party you have verified. See the section on what to share. Never send an ID selfie or bank login to anyone who is not your verified employer or bank.

9. The real shoot that never pays

What it looks like. The job exists and you work it, but the payment date keeps moving, the amount changes, or the client disappears. This is not always a scam. Sometimes it is simply a bad client.

Why it works. Models often do the work before anything is in writing, and payment commonly arrives after the shoot, so a delay is hard to challenge.

What to do. Get the fee, usage and payment date in writing before the shoot. Keep your messages and invoices. If an agency booked you, tell the agency early. If the date keeps moving, pause further jobs for that client. My honest guide to modeling in the UAE covers contracts and payment in more detail.

The 60-second test before you reply

Ask these seven questions before you answer an offer. If you can only say yes to some of them, pause.

  1. Can I confirm this sender independently? Find the company on its own website and use the contact details there, not the ones in the message.
  2. Is the client, job, date, rate, usage and payment date clear and in writing? If not, ask. A real client answers.
  3. Is anyone asking me for money? If yes, stop. This single question catches most scams.
  4. Is anyone asking for my passport, ID or bank details early? If yes, stop until the party is verified and the booking is real.
  5. Am I being rushed or told to keep this secret? Pressure and secrecy are tools of control, not of professionals.
  6. Does it involve working on a visit visa? If yes, see the visa section and ask the authority before you agree.
  7. Would I be comfortable telling a friend exactly where I will be and with whom? If not, change the plan, or do not go.

How to verify an agency or offer

Verification takes minutes, and a real business is rarely offended by it. Work through these in order.

  1. Start from the official website. Search for the company yourself, open its site, and contact it using the details published there. Ask whether the person who messaged you works there.
  2. Check the trade licence. Ask for the licence number. For a Dubai mainland company, the Dubai Department of Economy and Tourism (DET) provides an online business search. Free zone companies are checked with the issuing free zone authority, not the DET. Make sure the name, status and listed activity match the business you are dealing with. MOHRE also advises checking that a company is registered in the National Economic Register (Gulf News report on the MOHRE advice).
  3. Check the social accounts. Look at the exact username, when the account started, whether real clients and models tag it, and whether comments look genuine. Reverse-search profile photos of the “team” to see whether they are stolen.
  4. Ask for references. A real agency or photographer can point to named clients and models you can contact. Message one of them yourself.
  5. If there is a job or visa offer, verify it officially. MOHRE says an official offer carries a serial number and barcode that can be checked through the ministry’s website, app or call centre. For visa validity, Dubai-issued visas are checked with GDRFA and other emirates through the ICP eChannels platform (Gulf News report on the MOHRE advice). Use the contact details on the official sites, not the ones in a message. Note that a one-off booking from a client is not always an employment offer, so ask the authority which route applies to your case.
  6. Ask someone you trust. A photographer, a model who has worked here or a reputable agency can often tell you in one message whether a name is known.
  7. Give it time. If the offer cannot survive a day of checking, it was not an opportunity.
A useful habit

Move the conversation to a place you control. Reply through the official email or phone number you found yourself, and keep a record of everything. People who cannot be reached outside a direct message are worth a second look.

What to share and what to keep

Giving out the wrong thing too early is how a bad offer becomes a bigger problem.

  • Original passport. Do not hand it over as a deposit or for safekeeping. UAE labour law says an employer must not withhold a worker’s official documents, with a limited exception for visa processing and only with your consent, and it must be returned (Gulf News guide on employers holding passports). If it is kept, you can complain to MOHRE or contact your embassy.
  • Passport or ID copies. Send a copy only to a verified party that needs it for real paperwork, ideally by email you can trace, and write the purpose on the copy if you can.
  • Bank details. Share them only when you invoice a verified client for a confirmed job. Never share online banking logins or one-time codes with anyone.
  • Images. Public portfolio images and clean digitals are what a casting needs. Private images are never required. If you do a test shoot, agree in writing how the images may be used. My note on test shoots (TFP) explains what to agree.
  • Your location. Share the address and time of any meeting with someone you trust, and prefer a studio or office with other people around.
  • Under 18? A parent or guardian should handle every conversation and be present at every meeting. See my guide to photographing minor models in the UAE.

If you already paid or sent something

It happens to careful people. Act in this order, and do not blame yourself while you do.

  1. Stop. Do not send more money or documents, even if they promise to refund you after one more payment.
  2. Save the evidence. Screenshot messages, usernames, phone numbers, links, receipts and transfer details before you block anyone.
  3. Call your bank or card provider immediately. Tell them it was fraud. The sooner you do, the better your chances of stopping or tracing a transfer, though no one can promise a refund.
  4. Report it to the police. Use the channels in the next section.
  5. Report the account. Use the platform’s report tool so others are protected.
  6. If you sent a passport or ID copy, watch for misuse, and ask your bank and your embassy what extra protection they advise. If an original passport was taken, contact your embassy and MOHRE.
  7. If someone threatens you with your images, do not pay and do not send more. Keep the messages, report it and tell someone you trust.

Where to report

  • Dubai Police. Dubai Police say you can report fraud through the Dubai Police app, the eCrime platform for cybercrime, or by calling 901 (Gulf News report on the Dubai Police warning). The service is for crimes within Dubai.
  • Other emirates. Use the police channel for the emirate where you are. Check the official police website for the current number or app.
  • MOHRE. For a fake job offer, a recruitment fee or a withheld document, contact the Ministry of Human Resources and Emiratisation using the contact details on its official website.
  • Your embassy or consulate. If you are visiting, they can advise on lost documents, safety and local reporting.
  • The platform. Report the profile, chat or advert on Instagram, WhatsApp, Telegram or wherever you found it.

Contact details change. Always take them from the official government or police website, never from a message or a search advert.

If you are visiting for the season

Models who arrive for the season carry the most risk, because they are away from home, short of time and often relying on one contact. Three points matter most.

  • Visa and permission. A visit visa is not permission to work. Dubai Public Prosecution has warned that a visit-visa holder needs permission from the competent authority before working, and it lists penalties including a fine and deportation (Gulf News report of the advisory). The advisory does not say how unpaid test shoots are treated, so ask the authority or a lawyer before you travel and keep the answer in writing.
  • Do not travel on a promise. Nobody can guarantee bookings. Have two or three agencies or photographers you researched yourself, a return plan and money for the first weeks. My complete guide to modeling in the UAE covers this step by step.
  • Be careful with accommodation offers. A “model flat” arranged by an agency, with a deposit or a requirement to stay, should be verified like any other offer.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if a modeling agency in Dubai is real?
Look for a trade licence number you can verify, a real address, named clients you can confirm independently and written terms. Find the agency through its own website and contact it there, not through a direct message. A real agency earns commission from your bookings and does not need money from you before it represents you.
Do real modeling agencies charge fees to join?
A legitimate agency earns by taking a commission from the work it books for you, so you should not pay a signing, registration or portfolio fee to be represented. Some agencies offer optional paid services, such as photography, but these should be priced openly, optional and never a condition of being signed.
Can I work as a model in Dubai on a tourist visa?
A visit or tourist visa is not permission to work. Dubai Public Prosecution has warned that a visit-visa holder needs permission from the competent authority before working, and it lists penalties including a fine and deportation. Treat anyone who says to come as a tourist and sort the paperwork out later as a warning sign, and check with the authority or a qualified lawyer before you travel.
I paid a fake modeling agency. What should I do?
Stop all contact and do not send more money. Keep screenshots, receipts, account names and phone numbers as evidence. Tell your bank or card provider straight away, then report it to the police in your emirate. In Dubai you can use the Dubai Police app, the eCrime platform or call 901. Report the account to the platform too.
How do I report a modeling scam in the UAE?
In Dubai, Dubai Police accept reports through its app, the eCrime platform for cybercrime and the 901 number. Other emirates have their own police channels, so use the one for the emirate where you are. You can also report a fake job offer to MOHRE, and visiting models can contact their embassy.
Is a casting that asks for swimwear or lingerie photos a scam?
Not necessarily, because swimwear and lingerie are real categories in fashion and commercial work. The warning signs are the setting and the order of events: no named client, no written brief, a request for private images before any contract, a private apartment or hotel room, or pressure to keep it secret. Ask for the brief, the client and the usage in writing first.
Is a casting that arrives by Instagram or WhatsApp message real?
Sometimes, because real clients and photographers do find models on social media. Treat the message as a lead, not an offer. Check the sender through the company’s official website, confirm the client independently, and do not pay, click unfamiliar links or send documents until the terms are in writing.
